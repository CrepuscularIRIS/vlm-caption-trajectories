# C04 — Decompose Into Separately Rewarded Parts
_parent: **Decompose Into Separately Verifiable Parts** (`decompose_and_reward_parts`)_ · secondary: Substitute Cheap Verifiable Signals for Expensive Supervision · n=15 (oral 0, spotlight 2, poster 10, reject 3)

## Tactical pattern

The shared move is to break a compositional, multi-capability or multi-output task into parts — sub-skills, sub-tasks, coupled capability directions, cascaded branches, a proposal module and an executor, or heterogeneous output types — and give each part its own cheaply checkable reward inside one policy-gradient objective, so that a small training set or no full-task annotation suffices. The recurring reward shapes are: one rule-based check per sub-skill of a compositional output; cross-part rewards in which one part is scored by what another part can do with it (a generate-then-reconstruct cycle across two complementary capabilities, a localization branch rewarded by the frozen reasoning branch's success, a proposal policy rewarded by the executor's downstream score); per-output-type partial-credit rewards (rank agreement, kernelized closeness for continuous targets, semantic similarity for labels) that let heterogeneous targets share one stage; a cost or budget term that rewards outputs that are both correct and cheaper, anchored to a full-capacity reference; and behavior-category rewards in which a part (such as calling an auxiliary tool or requesting detail) is rewarded only on cases that genuinely need it. Training is typically a short warm-up that teaches only the format, then joint policy-gradient optimization, sometimes staged across parts to avoid gradient conflict. The load-bearing artifact is the factorization plus the per-part or cross-part reward, shown by ablation to beat both whole-task supervised training and separate per-part training.

## Step-by-Step

1. Factor the task into parts whose correctness is cheap to verify, and show why joint treatment is needed. Identify sub-skills, coupled capability directions, or a proposal/executor split, and run a pilot that compares joint optimization against separate or alternating training of the parts. The spotlight-tier papers led with such a pilot; a reject whose motivating study only showed that masking one part's gradient hurts performance was judged a logical leap, because degradation does not demonstrate interdependence.
2. Give each part a reward that checks that part, using cross-part signals where a part has no direct label. Rule-based per-sub-skill checks, per-output-type partial-credit rewards, and rewards defined by another part's downstream success (cycle reconstruction, executor score, frozen-branch feedback) all let the method avoid full-task annotation. Keep the reward grounded rather than self-scored where possible — a self-assessment term was flagged for endogeneity and gaming — and disclose any ground truth the reward needs (box or mask labels contradict a "no extra supervision" claim).
3. Couple and schedule the parts so they help rather than fight. Optimize them under one shared policy with cross-part or consistency terms, or stage them (localize first, then reason on the reduced input) with each stage rewarded by the other frozen part. Make the schedule consistent with the joint-optimization claim: a second task-specific stage after a unified stage was read as contradicting the claim that unified training is better. Merely re-labeling a standard optimizer with a weighted sum of heuristic terms reads as reward shaping, not decomposition.
4. Add cost or necessity terms only when the part has a real budget trade-off, and measure the cost honestly. Reward correct-and-cheaper outputs across varied budgets, or reward invoking an auxiliary part only on cases that need it, and report end-to-end compute (including encoders, retrievers and serial rollouts), not just the proxy (tokens kept, frames used). Reviewers repeatedly found efficiency gains overstated when the proxy diverged from measured latency or when auxiliary computation was excluded.
5. Ablate every part and compare against the obvious alternatives at equal data. Report single-reward and leave-one-reward-out variants, a no-warm-up and a supervised-only baseline, the training-free version of the same heuristic, and baselines trained on the same new data; test more than one backbone. The rejects in this cluster gained only marginally over a standard multi-step optimizer or trailed specialist systems, with narrow scope and one backbone — so the decomposed rewards must earn clear gains, not a fraction of a point.

## When to pick this one

Reach for this when the target task is a composition of separable capabilities (or a pipeline of proposal and execution) whose individual parts can be checked cheaply by a rule, a reference, or another part's success, and when full-task supervised data is scarce. If the parts cannot be verified independently, or the only signal is the system grading itself, the decomposition collapses into an ordinary outcome reward with extra heuristic terms.

## Tactical failure mode

Papers attempting this tactic fail most often when the decomposition is cosmetic: an unchanged policy-gradient optimizer with a sum of straightforward reward terms, presented as a new method, earning small gains over a standard multi-step baseline. A second recurring shape is a weak motivating study (gradient masking presented as proof of interdependence) or a heuristic per-token importance score with many hyperparameters and unreported overhead. A third is scope: one task, one backbone, a lead over generalists but not specialists, and degradation elsewhere. Across accepted papers too, reviewers flagged unequal comparisons (distillation from a much larger model, baselines not trained on the new data) and efficiency claims that ignore auxiliary compute. Self-check: does a pilot show joint optimization beats separate training; is each part's reward verifiable without the system grading itself; and does each reward survive a leave-one-out ablation with a clear, budget-honest gain.

## Examples
### Accepted lessons
- Training both directions of a complementary capability pair under one policy, with a reward that checks whether one direction's output can be reconstructed by the other, lets each capability reinforce the other — and a pilot showing joint beats separate or alternating training is what makes the claim credible.
- Replacing exact-match rewards with per-output-type partial credit (rank agreement, kernelized closeness for continuous targets, similarity for labels) lets heterogeneous outputs be optimized in a single stage.
- Splitting a task into one rule-checkable reward per sub-skill can replace large full-task annotation with a few thousand samples, as long as each reward is ablated.
- Rewarding a proposal module by the downstream score of a separate executor, blended with coverage and format terms, trains information gathering without trajectory supervision.
- Sampling rollouts at several resource budgets per group and rewarding those that are both correct and cheaper turns efficiency into an end-to-end objective, anchored by a full-capacity reference.
- Rewarding an auxiliary action only on training cases that genuinely lack sufficient information (categorized in advance) discourages indiscriminate use of the action.

### Reject lessons
- Decomposing a multi-turn trajectory into independent single-step problems so the intermediate action gets its own dense reward added only about half a point over standard multi-step optimization, and the improvement was judged insignificant.
- Combining a matching-based supervised loss on one part's tokens with order-invariant rewards was seen as an engineering combination with narrow scope that still trailed specialist systems.
- Reweighting two token types by uncertainty and input-sensitivity scores rested on a motivating study judged a logical leap and a heuristic, hyperparameter-heavy score evaluated on one backbone with minor gains.
