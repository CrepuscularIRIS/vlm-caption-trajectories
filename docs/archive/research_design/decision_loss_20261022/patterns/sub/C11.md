# C11 — Utility-Measured Training Data Selection
_parent: **Measure Each Example's Utility and Select** (`measure_example_utility`)_ · secondary: none · n=12 (oral 0, spotlight 0, poster 6, reject 6)

## Tactical pattern

The shared move is to question the assumption that more training data is better for an already strong pretrained system, show that the existing pool is redundant or partly harmful, and then estimate each example's (or each source's) real contribution so the pool can be selected or reweighted — typically demonstrating that a small subset matches or beats training on everything. The utility estimate takes recurring forms: a cheap closed-form score derived from signals already present in the data (label mixture and label reliability, annotator-uncertainty windows, proxy-model loss or perplexity); a small learned scorer trained on a sample and then applied to the whole pool, sometimes coupling importance with diversity; gradient-based influence of an instance on its own task, with a budget allocated across tasks by estimated difficulty; per-example or per-domain weights learned by bi-level optimization against a held-out objective that emulates test-time use; perturbation sensitivity of a proxy model used to locate vulnerable regions of the distribution; cluster-level utility measured by actually training on clusters and regressing it on cheap indicators; or a heuristic search over subsets guided by a multi-model quality profile. The load-bearing artifact is the utility estimator and the evidence that it ranks examples better than random or simple heuristics at matched budget; the headline result is a fraction of the data reaching full-data performance at lower cost.

## Step-by-Step

1. Establish the redundancy or harm empirically, with training dynamics controlled. Show saturation under random subsampling, non-monotonic performance versus data size, or a stratum of examples (high annotator disagreement, unreliable labels) that measurably hurts accuracy or calibration. Hold training steps and convergence fixed across data sizes; a "less is more" curve that could be explained by overtraining or uncontrolled schedules is the reason scaling-law challenges get rejected.
2. Explain why some examples carry more learning signal, and derive the utility estimate from that explanation. The strongest submissions turn an analysis (for example, which label configurations produce informative gradients, or which uncertainty range helps versus harms) into a score; a pipeline assembled from existing clustering, filtering and sampling components, searched exhaustively, reads as engineering even when it works. Where the estimate needs extra machinery (bi-level weights, influence gradients, external descriptions), justify each piece and its stabilizers.
3. Keep the estimator cheap relative to the training it saves, and audit its dependencies. Prefer signals already in the data or a lightweight scorer trained on a small sample; report selection cost versus random sampling and versus full training. Flag any requirement for a held-out set aligned with the test distribution, task labels, access to the target evaluation set, or a closed external model to write descriptions — reviewers treat each of these as limiting applicability and as a possible source of bias or leakage.
4. Compare against strong selection baselines at matched budgets, not only against random. Include established influence-based, meta-reweighting, pseudo-labeling, uncertainty or active-learning and recent selection methods; give baselines the same augmentations and evaluation signals your method uses; check training/evaluation source overlap. Report multiple seeds and significance — sub-point or roughly one-point average gains over random, from single runs, are routinely discounted.
5. Show the selection rule generalizes and characterize what it keeps. Evaluate across more than one backbone, more than one training pool and different budgets, including out-of-distribution tests; show sensitivity to window thresholds, cluster counts and sample ratios; and describe what selected versus filtered data look like, so the claimed mechanism (robustness, difficulty, reliability) is distinguished from plain difficulty-based sampling. Scope any claim about "breaking" scaling behavior to the families tested.

## When to pick this one

Reach for this when you are fine-tuning a strong pretrained system on a large, heterogeneous or noisily labeled pool, there is evidence of saturation or harm from some examples, and you can articulate why particular examples carry more signal — ideally in a form that yields a near-free score. If the only argument is that a clever pipeline beats random by a point on one model and one dataset, or the estimator costs about as much as training on everything, the tactic will not clear the bar.

## Tactical failure mode

The dominant failure is the confounded redundancy claim: a subset beats full data, but training steps, convergence or schedule were not controlled, so overtraining rather than redundancy may explain it. The second is component assembly without insight — clustering, quota allocation, influence, filtering or bi-level reweighting stacked from existing techniques (or a direct refinement of one prior reweighting method) with small gains over random that may not justify the complexity. The third is the self-defeating cost: gradient-based scoring with a warm-up reference model, or bi-level optimization needing several stabilizers, whose overhead approaches full training. The fourth is weak and narrow evidence: one model, one dataset, random sampling as the main baseline, single runs, no significance, plus dependencies on test-aligned held-out sets, task labels or external closed-model descriptions. A candidate can self-check: is the redundancy shown with training dynamics controlled, does the score come from an explanation rather than a search over components, and does it beat strong selection baselines at matched budget across backbones at a cost well below full training.

## Examples
### Accepted lessons
- A gradient-variance analysis showing that mixed positive/negative labels and reliable positive labels drive learning can be turned into a zero-cost per-example score that matches full-data performance with about a tenth of the data.
- Stratifying examples by human annotator uncertainty reveals that high-disagreement samples hurt accuracy and calibration, and a mid-uncertainty window then decides which samples to train on and which to pseudo-label from a small seed.
- A lightweight scorer trained on a small random sample under a joint importance-plus-diversity objective can rank the whole pool in one pass at the lowest selection compute among competitors.
- Learning per-domain weights by differentiating a held-out loss that emulates test-time scoring through the model's training addresses uneven-quality mixtures, provided overlap between training sources and evaluation sets is filtered.
- Using a proxy model's inconsistency under input perturbations to locate vulnerable regions, then prioritizing training examples near them, gives a gradient-free selector faster than gradient-based ones.

### Reject lessons
- A "small subset beats full data" result whose training steps and convergence are not controlled is read as confounded by overtraining, not as evidence of redundancy.
- A multi-stage selector assembled from existing clustering, quota and sampling techniques that beats simple random sampling by only one or two points is judged too complex for its benefit.
- Moving a prior domain-level bi-level reweighting scheme to the instance level, with several stabilizers of unestablished convergence and a required test-aligned meta set, is seen as limited novelty.
- Influence-based scoring that needs a warm-up reference model and full gradients can approach the cost of full training, undermining the efficiency claim.
- A selector that depends on descriptions generated by an external closed model and on knowing the target evaluation set's demand type is discounted for bias, prompt sensitivity and limited applicability to unseen tasks.
